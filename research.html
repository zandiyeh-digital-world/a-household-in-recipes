Very Plain Bread Pudding

Ingredients.—Odd pieces of crust or crumb of bread: to every [quart] allow ½ teaspoonful of salt, 1 teaspoonful of grated nutmeg, 3 oz. of [moist] sugar, ½ lb. of currants, 1½ oz. of butter.

Mode.—Break the bread into small pieces, and pour on them as [much] boiling water as will soak them well. Let these stand till the water [is quite] cool; then press it out, and mash the bread with a fork until it [is] quite free from lumps. Measure this pulp, and to every quart stir [in the] salt, nutmeg, sugar and currants in the above proportion; mix all [well] together and put it into a well-buttered pie-dish. Smooth the [surface] with the back of a spoon, and place the butter in small pieces over [the] top, bake in a moderate oven for an hour and a half, and serve very [hot.] Boiling milk substituted for the boiling water would very much [improve] this pudding.

Time.—1½ hour. Average Cost, 6d., exclusive of the bread.

Sufficient for 6 or 7 persons.
Seasonable at any time.

Source evidence

  • The recipe explicitly reuses odd crusts or crumbs.
  • It prices the pudding at 6d. excluding the bread, for six or seven people.
  • Milk is proposed as an improvement on water. The recipe contains currants, nutmeg, sugar and butter even in its plain form.

Curatorial interpretation

This is direct evidence of an ideal of household thrift and of accounting that treats existing bread differently from new purchases. It is not proof of working-class consumption. The optional milk also makes visible a choice between a cheaper base and an enriched version, though the price of that substitution is not supplied.

Consumer and preparer

Class unassigned. Economy and reuse are explicit; the actual consumer’s occupation, income and social position are unknown.

Unknown. The soaking, pressing, mashing and oven tending could be performed by a household member or paid worker; the recipe does not identify one.

Labour, cost and affordability

Ninety minutes is the stated baking time, not a measurement of total labour. Cooling, preparation, heating the oven, fuel use and cleaning are not separately costed or timed.

Sixpence is an incomplete dish cost: bread is explicitly excluded. Do not label it free food, a complete meal, or an observed purchase. The printed estimate does not separately identify fuel, equipment or labour charges.

No recipe-specific income evidence. Read with the manual’s staffing scale on p. 7, not as a dish assigned to one income band.

Evidence limits

  • Find a clearer image of p. 824 in the same edition to resolve bracketed outer-margin wording.
  • Locate a dated household account or menu demonstrating actual use; distinguish the cost of existing bread from newly bought bread.
  • Investigate how the oven was heated and whether its fuel served several dishes.

Raised Pie of Poultry or Game

Exact Internet Archive source page ↗ · Local source image

Raised Pie of Poultry or Game

Pâté froid de Gibier—de Volaille.

Ingredients.—To every lb. of flour allow ½ lb. of butter, ½ pint [of] water, the yolks of 2 eggs, ½ teaspoonful of salt (these are for the [crust]); 1 large fowl or pheasant, a few slices of veal cutlet, a few slices of [dressed] ham, forcemeat, seasoning of nutmeg, allspice, pepper and salt, gravy.

Mode.—Make a stiff short-crust with the above proportion of [butter,] flour, water, and eggs, and work it up very smoothly; butter a raised-[pie] mould, as shown in No. 1670, and line it with the paste. Previously [to] making the crust, bone the fowl, or whatever bird is intended to be [used]; lay it, breast downwards, upon a cloth, and season the inside well [with] pounded mace, allspice, pepper, and salt; then spread over it a layer [of] forcemeat, then a layer of seasoned veal, and then one of ham, and [then] another layer of forcemeat, and roll the fowl over, making the skin [meet] at the back. Line the pie with forcemeat, put in the fowl, and fill up [the] cavities with slices of seasoned veal [and] ham and forcemeat; wet the edges of [the] pie, put on the cover, pinch the [edges] together with the paste-pincers, and [decorate] it with leaves; brush it over with beaten [yolk] of egg, and bake in a moderate oven [for] 4 hours. In the meantime, make a good strong gravy from the bones, pour it through a funnel into the hole at the top; cover this hole with a small leaf, and the pie, when cold, will be ready for use. Let it be remembered that the gravy must be considerably reduced before it is poured into the pie, as, when cold, it should form a firm jelly, and not be the least degree in a liquid state. The recipe is suitable for all kinds of poultry or game, using one or more birds, according to the size of the pie intended to be made; but the birds must always be boned. Truffles, mushrooms, &c., added to this pie, make it much nicer; and, to enrich it, lard the fleshy parts of the poultry or game with thin strips of bacon. This method of forming raised pies in a mould is generally called a timbale, and has the advantage of being more easily made than one where the paste is raised by the hands; the crust, besides, being eatable.

Time.—Large pie, 4 hours. Average Cost, 5s. 6d.

No fixed number of diners is given; the recipe allows one or more birds according to pie size.
Seasonable, with poultry, all the year; with game, from September to March.

Source evidence

  • The method requires boning, pastry work, layering, decoration and reduced gravy.
  • The printed cost is 5s. 6d.; a large pie is given four hours. No fixed serving count is supplied.
  • A mould, paste-pincers, funnel and oven are named. Truffles, mushrooms and bacon are optional enrichments.

Curatorial interpretation

Layered meats, decoration and specialist operations make skilled culinary display central to this recipe. They establish resource-intensive preparation, without identifying actual diners, an upper-class monopoly or the employment of a professional cook. The manual describes moulding as easier than raising the pastry by hand.

Consumer and preparer

Possible association with resource-intensive entertaining is a curatorial hypothesis. Actual class and household income remain unassigned.

The operations require skill, but the preparer’s employment is unknown. A cook or household member is possible; an individual wage cannot be attached to this recipe.

Labour, cost and affordability

The four-hour figure describes baking. Boning, forcemeat, pastry, gravy-making and cooling add processes without measured durations; some occur in parallel. Four hours is therefore the stated baking time, not a measure of total labour.

5s. 6d. equals 66 old pence. Bird number and pie size can vary; no defensible per-person price is available. The book does not itemise the cost of optional enrichments, utensils, fuel or labour here.

For context, p. 7 recommends a cook, housemaid and foot-boy for about £500 annual household income. This is a staffing recommendation, not evidence that £500 households served this pie.

Evidence limits

  • Resolve the mould cross-reference and document the relevant equipment illustration.
  • Find a dated menu or account showing this specific pie or a securely comparable one.
  • Trace forcemeat and gravy components before attempting a complete ingredient-cost reconstruction.

Lemon Jelly

Exact Internet Archive source page ↗ · Local source image

Lemon Jelly

Gelée au Citron.

Ingredients.—6 lemons, ¾ lb. lump sugar, 1 pint of water, 1¼ oz. of gelatine, ¼ pint of sherry.

Mode.—Peel three of the lemons, pour half a pint of boiling water on the rind, and let it infuse for half an hour; put the sugar, gelatine, and half a pint of water into a lined saucepan, simmer these ingredients for 10 minutes; then put in the strained lemon juice, the strained infusion of rind, and bring the whole to the point of boiling; skim well, add the wine, and run the jelly through a bag; pour it into a mould that has been wetted or soaked in water; put it in ice, if convenient, where let it remain until required for table. Previously to adding the lemon-juice to the other ingredients, ascertain that it is very nicely strained, as, if this is not properly attended to, it is liable to make the jelly thick and muddy. As this jelly is very pale, and almost colourless, it answers very well for moulding with a jelly of any bright hue; for instance, half a jelly bright red, and the other half made of the above, would have a very good effect. Lemon jelly may also be made with patent gelatine, allowing the juice of three lemons to every pint.

Time.—Altogether, 1 hour. Average Cost, 2s. 2d.

Sufficient to fill 1½-pint mould.
Seasonable at any time.

Source evidence

  • The recipe uses six lemons, sugar, gelatine and sherry, with a printed estimate of 2s. 2d.
  • A lined saucepan, straining bag and mould are required by the method; ice is expressly conditional, not mandatory.
  • Clarity and two-colour moulding are explicit aesthetic concerns. Yield is a 1½-pint mould, not a specified number of diners.

Curatorial interpretation

Repeated filtering and the suggested colour contrast show appearance as an objective of preparation. The optional ice invites investigation of cooling access, while sherry and commercial gelatine open questions about household purchasing. These are evidence-led research directions; neither the recipe nor its price proves that only wealthy people ate jelly. No specific trade origin is established for these ingredients in this copy.

Consumer and preparer

Decorative presentation is explicit; an exclusive upper-class association is not established. Actual consumers and income are unknown.

Unknown. Straining, skimming and moulding show work and skill, but do not identify whether the worker was paid.

Labour, cost and affordability

The book states one hour altogether, yet also tells the cook to leave the mould until required. Record the printed time separately from unknown setting and cooling time; do not present one hour as a verified ready-to-eat duration.

2s. 2d. equals 26 old pence for the recipe’s mould yield. No per-person price can be calculated without an assumed portion size. Ice is optional; possession of an ice-house cannot be inferred.

No income is attached to the dish. The manual’s £20–£30 annual cook’s cash-wage range with specified allowances is contextual, not a measure of the consumer’s budget or the worker’s total resources.

Evidence limits

  • Research local ice supply, commercial gelatine and mould prices for a clearly defined date and place.
  • Find household accounts recording purchases of sherry or gelatine, and menus showing actual jelly service.
  • Investigate ingredients’ trade histories using dated supplier and customs evidence rather than assigning origins from the recipe alone.

Social and economic framework

Whose income? Whose work?

The book’s p. 7 places household income alongside recommended numbers and kinds of servants. Its wage table also ranks paid domestic work. These are prescriptions printed in a household manual, not a survey of actual households. They can reveal an imagined social order without identifying who ate the three dishes.

Rounded shares may not sum to exactly 100%.

Maraschino Jelly

The referenced recipe is Liqueur Jelly, no. 2006, p. 938. Its method names Maraschino among suitable liqueurs. The recipe’s 2s. 6d. average cost with best gelatine matches this menu line; its stated yield is a quart mould.

Recipe 2006 requires heating and skimming syrup and gelatine, straining lemon juice, filtering the jelly through a bag, setting a mould in ice, and unmoulding. Its ten-minute time line applies only to boiling the sugar and water, not making and setting the whole jelly.

A saucepan, filtering bag and mould are explicitly named, and the method calls for ice. The quoted copper moulds elsewhere are examples from an equipment list, not proof that copper was necessary or used for this dinner.

Unitemised resources

Fuel and heat. The menu has no separate fuel line. Cooking methods require heat, but the stove, fuel, consumption and price for this dinner are unknown. No fuel surcharge has been invented.

Work and service. Wages, preparation, serving and cleaning are not separately itemised in the dinner table. The source does not define a complete accounting boundary, so absence of an itemised charge is not proof it was excluded from every estimate.

Cooling and storage. The jelly method calls for ice and the turbot starts with cooked remains. The menu provides no separate ice or storage charge and no equipment inventory for the hypothetical household.

Earlier preparation. Stock and already cooked fish carry prior inputs. The printed menu does not tell us how those inputs were allocated between meals. A recipe’s finishing time cannot recover their complete cost.

The original reports separate the cost of the batch from money received. They correct the financial reading while leaving unit sales open.

A batch cost in historical US dollars. It is not a count of 1,351 copies sold.

What is resolved, and what remains open?

Resolved for this account: cost, sales receipts and the reported role of donations are distinct. The entry $1,351.01 is a cost; it supplies no evidence for 1,351 copies sold. The $333.31 is described as covered, rather than remaining an uncovered loss.

Still open: the 1922 history’s assertion that 3,000 copies were sold is not verified by these cash reports. A batch of 3,000 and receipts of $1,017.70 cannot establish unit sales without evidence of prices actually received, free distribution, credit and unsold stock. The November report has not been identified as the exact item cited as Eaton 1910 in Williams’s bibliography.

Interpretation: cookbook publishing required a combination of trading receipts and voluntary contributions. A political use for a book can coexist with sales that did not cover its stated cost.
